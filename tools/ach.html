Scenario — The Bakery Dilemma A few days before Thanksgiving, a bakery producing specially prepared bread for several grocery chains begins losing batches — loaves coming out burned and worthless. Three separate batches overbaked across two crews, each using their own set of five gas ovens. A serviceman who examined the ovens noted that a gas pressure problem would affect all ovens simultaneously — not just two. Three weeks prior, employees were denied additional benefits; two weeks prior, Michael was fired amid employee unrest. Frank (Crew 2) was seen meeting Michael in the parking lot the day before. That morning, a delayed flour shipment from a new supplier arrived — and only batches made with the new flour overbaked.
How to Rate Each Cell — Quick Reference

N/A
Not Applicable — The evidence comes from a completely different causal domain and has no logical connection to this hypothesis at all.
N/A vs. I — the key distinction: Ask yourself, "Could this evidence even theoretically affect this hypothesis?" If no (e.g., employee anger cannot cause gas pressure to change) → N/A. If yes but the evidence works against it → I.

Key Takeaways
1
The primary objective of ACH is to minimize confirmation bias by identifying the hypothesis with the least inconsistent evidence, not the one with the most support.
2
A single piece of highly discriminating evidence can fundamentally alter an analytical conclusion, turning circumstantial indicators into irrelevant data points.
3
Evidence that relates exclusively to human emotion or motivation provides zero analytical value when evaluated against mechanical or material failures.

💬 Discussion — E9 & the Sabotage Hypothesis

You might wonder whether E9 could be rated Consistent with Sabotage — if employees deliberately contaminated the new flour supply. That is creative thinking, but structured analysis requires staying within the available evidence: Frank (the prime suspect) successfully baked his first batch using the old flour. Arguing that a coordinated effort specifically targeted only the new flour adds a complex, multi-step assumption with no supporting evidence in the scenario. When a simpler explanation — defective supplier goods — carries zero inconsistencies and requires fewer assumptions, it is the mathematically and logically superior hypothesis.

▶ The Key Insight in This Scenario

Pay close attention to Evidence Item 9. Before E9, Sabotage appears highly likely because of the volume of motive-based evidence (E4–E7). E9 shows how a single piece of highly diagnostic evidence can completely overturn a leading hypothesis by exposing a logical inconsistency — and that is the core value of the ACH method.

📊 Advanced ACH — What Comes Next

The version practiced here uses a simplified three-state rating: C / I / N/A. Full professional ACH adds two additional tools that advanced analysts use once they are comfortable with the fundamentals:

  • CC / II (Conclusive ratings): A five-point scale — CC (Conclusively Consistent), C, N/A, I, II (Conclusively Inconsistent) — lets analysts flag evidence that is so strong or so definitive that it essentially rules a hypothesis in or out on its own. E9 in this scenario would be a strong candidate for an II rating against H1 and H2.
  • Evidence weights: Not all evidence is equally reliable or diagnostic. Weighted ACH assigns each evidence item a credibility multiplier (e.g., Low / Medium / High) so that a firsthand, verified source contributes more to the count than rumor or inference. In this scenario, the flour correlation finding (E9) would carry far higher weight than circumstantial employee grievance reports (E4–E7).

These features add analytical precision but also add complexity. Master the fundamentals first — a well-reasoned C/I/N/A matrix is more valuable than a poorly understood weighted one.
